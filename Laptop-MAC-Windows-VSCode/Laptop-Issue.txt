AI Is Increasing the Velocity of Engineering — But Can the Ecosystem Keep Up?

AI is increasing the velocity of engineering.

We can now generate and explore ideas, concepts, code, patches and implementations much faster.

That's a huge opportunity.

But increased velocity also makes quality, architectural alignment and organizational priorities even more important.

Not every idea needs to become another application, service or infrastructure component. Ideas and implementations still need to align with company strategy, organizational goals, architecture principles, security requirements and available resources.

Otherwise, the bottleneck simply moves downstream:

Ideas → Development → Git → CI/CD → Security → Review → Architecture → Infrastructure → Operations → Monitoring

Every stage — including the teams and resources supporting those stages — can potentially become overloaded.

I recently heard a team describe an “unprecedented increase in received IDFs” this year.

That made me think that the challenge isn't necessarily the number of ideas.

The real challenge is:

How do we maintain quality, focus and alignment when our ability to create increases so dramatically?

And this brings me to a rather simple example from my own experience over the last couple of days.

My developer laptop became the system I had to troubleshoot. 🙂

As developers and architects, we regularly troubleshoot performance problems in applications, services and infrastructure.

This time, the system was my own workstation.

Instead of immediately assuming that I needed higher-end hardware, I followed the same approach I would use for a production system:

Measure → Understand → Identify the right culprit → Optimize → Measure again → Scale when necessary

Initially, I found VS Code processes consuming extremely high CPU. After investigation and some configuration changes, that particular load dropped significantly.

But that wasn't the whole story.

Further investigation pointed to filesystem activity, OneDrive synchronization, File Provider processes and macOS background services as additional contributors.

The interesting part was that the right culprit wasn't necessarily the first culprit I found.

And I think there is a broader lesson here.

Whether we are troubleshooting a laptop, application, service, platform or enterprise infrastructure, the principle is similar:

Don't just react to the symptom. Find the right source of the load.

Optimize and scale — we need both.

If the workload genuinely requires more CPU, memory, infrastructure or capacity, scale it.

But when resources are constrained, or before making a significant investment, it is worth understanding whether there are unnecessary workloads, configuration issues, duplicated activities or background processes contributing to the problem.

In my case, spending time investigating the workstation made a noticeable difference.

I'm sharing the complete troubleshooting journey, measurements, fixes, VS Code configuration, filesystem/OneDrive observations, useful commands and developer-workstation best practices separately.

Hopefully it helps someone facing a similar problem — particularly when an immediate high-end laptop upgrade isn't possible because of budget or infrastructure constraints.

Sometimes the answer is to scale.
Sometimes the answer is to optimize.
And sometimes, the most important step is simply finding the right culprit first.
